# Chapter 8 Force and Pressure

## 8.1 Force: A Push or a Pull
Opening a door, kicking a football and lifting a school bag all involve a push or a pull. In science, a push or a pull on an object is called a force. A force arises from the interaction between at least two objects. The SI unit of force is the newton (N).

## 8.2 Forces Due to an Interaction
Forces applied in the same direction add up. If two friends push a heavy box in the same direction, the net force is the sum of their forces. Forces applied in opposite directions subtract: in a tug of war, if both teams pull equally hard the rope does not move. The net force is zero when balanced.

## 8.3 Exploring Forces
A force has both a size (magnitude) and a direction. If the direction or the magnitude changes, its effect also changes.

## 8.4 A Force Can Change the State of Motion
A force can make a stationary object move, stop a moving object, change its speed or change its direction of motion. When a goalkeeper stops a ball, the force he applies brings the ball to rest. A cyclist pedalling harder increases the speed of the bicycle.

## 8.5 Force Can Change the Shape of an Object
Pressing a ball of dough, squeezing a rubber ball or stretching a rubber band changes its shape. Some objects regain their shape when the force is removed (a rubber band), others do not (a lump of clay).

## 8.6 Contact Forces
Muscular force is the force produced by the action of our muscles, such as when we lift a bucket. Friction is the force that opposes motion between two surfaces in contact; it is why a ball rolling on the ground slowly comes to rest. Both need contact with the object.

## 8.7 Non-contact Forces
Magnetic force: a magnet attracts iron without touching it, and like poles repel. Electrostatic force: a plastic comb rubbed in dry hair attracts small bits of paper; the force exerted by a charged body is electrostatic force. Gravitational force: every object in the universe attracts every other object. The force with which the Earth pulls objects towards itself is called gravity. That is why a ball thrown up comes back down.

## 8.8 Pressure
The force acting on a unit area of a surface is called pressure.
$$\text{Pressure} = \frac{\text{Force}}{\text{Area on which it acts}}$$
The SI unit of pressure is the pascal (Pa), where $1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N/m}^2$. For the same force, a smaller area gives a larger pressure. This is why the cutting edge of a knife is sharp, nails have pointed tips, and porters place a round cloth on their heads to spread the load over a larger area.

Example: A box weighing 600 N rests on a floor on a face of area $2\ \text{m}^2$. Pressure $= \frac{600}{2} = 300$ Pa. If it is turned onto a face of area $0.5\ \text{m}^2$, pressure $= 1200$ Pa.

## 8.9 Pressure Exerted by Liquids and Gases
Liquids exert pressure on the walls of their container, and the pressure increases with depth. That is why the wall of a dam is made thicker at the bottom. Water spurts farther from a hole low in a bottle than from a hole near the top. Gases also exert pressure on the walls of their container, which is why a balloon stays inflated.

## 8.10 Atmospheric Pressure
The envelope of air around the Earth is called the atmosphere, and the pressure it exerts is atmospheric pressure. A rubber sucker sticks to a smooth surface because the air pressure outside is greater than the pressure inside. We are not crushed by this pressure because the pressure inside our bodies is equal to it and cancels it.

Keywords: force, net force, friction, gravity, magnetic force, electrostatic force, pressure, pascal, atmospheric pressure.
