You study a chapter on Monday. By Friday, it's mostly gone. You study it again. A week later, gone again.

This isn't because you're bad at remembering. It's because memory fades on a pretty predictable curve, and the way to beat it is also pretty predictable: review things at gradually longer gaps. That's spaced repetition.

## The forgetting curve, briefly

In the 1880s a researcher named Hermann Ebbinghaus tested his own memory on lists of nonsense syllables and noticed something that's held up ever since. We forget quickly at first, then more slowly. A big chunk of new material is gone within a day or two if we don't revisit it.

But each time you review, the curve gets flatter. You forget more slowly. So the second review can wait longer than the first, the third longer than the second, and so on.

In practice, this means a few short reviews spread out beat one long session every time.

## A schedule for exams that are a month or more away

After you learn a topic for the first time (day 0), review it on:

| Review | When |
| --- | --- |
| 1st | Day 1 (the next day) |
| 2nd | Day 3 |
| 3rd | Day 7 |
| 4th | Day 14 |
| 5th | Day 30 |

Each review should be quick, usually 5 to 15 minutes, and it should be a test, not a reread. Close your notes, try to recall the key points, answer a few questions, then check. (Here's why [active recall](/blog/active-recall-study-method/) matters so much.)

If a review goes badly, don't move on to the longer gap. Do the topic again in a day or two, then continue.

## A schedule for when the exam is in a week

You can still use spacing, just with tighter gaps:

| Review | When |
| --- | --- |
| 1st | Same day, a few hours later |
| 2nd | Next day |
| 3rd | Two or three days later |
| 4th | The day before the exam |

It won't be as strong as a month of spacing, but it's far better than studying each topic once and hoping.

## How to keep track without going mad

The hard part of spaced repetition isn't the idea. It's remembering what to review on which day once you've got twenty topics going. A few options:

**A paper calendar.** When you finish a topic, write its name on the next four review dates. Each morning, look at today's box. Low tech, works fine.

**A spreadsheet.** One row per topic, columns for the date learned and each review. Tick them off.

**Flashcard apps.** Tools like Anki schedule cards for you based on how well you answered. Great if you're willing to make the cards.

**Let the study tool handle it.** In [Lumina IQ](/), reviews are scheduled automatically from the practice questions you've already answered. Topics you're shaky on come back sooner, topics you nailed come back later. You just open it and do what's due.

## What a review session looks like

Say you've got 30 minutes and four topics due for review. A good session might go:

1. Topic A: write down everything you remember (3 minutes), check your notes (2 minutes).
2. Topic B: answer five practice questions, check answers.
3. Topic C: explain it out loud, then look up the bit you blanked on.
4. Topic D: flashcards, honest answers only.

Then go learn something new. Reviews first, new material second, is a good habit because reviews are the thing you'll skip if you leave them for later.

## Mistakes that break the system

- **Skipping reviews when you feel confident.** Feeling sure is exactly when the curve drops quietly. Do the review; it'll be fast.
- **Making reviews too long.** If every review takes an hour, you'll stop doing them. Keep them short.
- **Only reviewing by rereading.** Spacing plus rereading is okay. Spacing plus testing is much better.
- **Starting the night before.** Spacing needs days. Even three or four days helps a lot.

## Does it really make a difference?

Yes. Spacing is one of the most reliable findings in memory research, and it's been shown with school students, university students, medical trainees and language learners. Combined with self-testing, it's about as close as studying gets to a free upgrade.

The catch is that it requires starting a bit earlier than most of us want to. If you need help planning backwards from the exam date, our post on [how to make a study plan for exams](/blog/exam-study-plan/) walks through it step by step.

## In one sentence

Review each topic the next day, a few days later, a week later and a few weeks later, and test yourself each time instead of rereading.
