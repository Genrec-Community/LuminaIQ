Every exam season starts the same way. You open the syllabus, count the chapters, count the days, and do some quiet maths that doesn't work out. There's more to cover than there is time.

The good news is that almost nobody who does well in exams actually "covers everything" evenly. They cover the right things first, and they spend most of their time checking what they know instead of rereading what they've already seen. That's the whole trick. The rest of this post is just how to do it.

## Start with the paper, not the textbook

Before you read a single page, find out what the exam actually asks. Get two or three past papers (your seniors, your teacher, the board website, a university portal, wherever). Then go through them with a pencil and mark:

- Which topics come up every single year
- How marks are split between short answers, long answers and MCQs
- What kind of question it is: define this, explain that, solve this, compare these

You'll usually find that a handful of chapters carry a huge chunk of the marks. In a lot of school and university papers, it's something like a third of the syllabus producing more than half the questions. That's where you start.

This feels like cheating. It isn't. It's just reading the instructions before building the furniture.

## Sort the syllabus into three piles

Take every topic and put it into one of three groups:

1. **Must know.** Comes up often, carries big marks, or everything else depends on it.
2. **Should know.** Shows up sometimes, medium weight.
3. **Nice to know.** Rare, low marks, or so niche you'd be unlucky to see it.

Be honest here. The goal isn't to skip pile three forever. It's to make sure that if you run out of time (and you might), the time you lost was on the stuff that mattered least.

One more thing: inside "must know", look for foundations. If chapter 7 makes no sense without chapter 3, chapter 3 goes first even if it's worth fewer marks. Foundations make everything after them faster.

## Stop rereading. Start testing yourself.

This is the part most people skip, because it feels bad.

Rereading notes feels productive. The words look familiar, you nod along, and it seems like you know it. But recognising something on the page is a very different skill from pulling it out of your head in an exam hall with nothing in front of you. The exam tests the second skill.

So after you read a section once, close the book and try to answer questions on it. Write down everything you remember. Explain it out loud to an empty chair. Do a past paper question cold. You'll get things wrong, and that is exactly the point. Every wrong answer shows you a gap while there's still time to fix it.

Researchers call this [active recall](/blog/active-recall-study-method/), and it's one of the most consistently supported findings in learning science. It's also the habit that separates people who "studied a lot" from people who remember what they studied.

## Space it out, even a little

If you study a topic on Monday and never look at it again, most of it will be gone by the following week. That's not a personal failing. It's how memory works for everyone.

The fix is boring and effective: come back to it. A quick review the next day, again a few days later, then once more before the exam. Each time it takes less effort because there's more left over. We wrote a simple version of this in our post on [spaced repetition schedules for students](/blog/spaced-repetition-schedule/).

If your exam is in a week, you can still do this. The gaps are just shorter.

## A day that actually works

Here's a rough shape for a full study day during exam season. Adjust it, but keep the ratios:

- **Morning (fresh brain):** new material from your "must know" pile. Read once, carefully, then close the book and test yourself.
- **After lunch:** practice questions or a timed past paper section. Mark it honestly.
- **Late afternoon:** fix what went wrong. Go back only to the parts you missed.
- **Evening:** short review of yesterday's topics. Flashcards, a quick quiz, or just writing out key points from memory.

Notice that "reading" is maybe a quarter of the day. Most of it is retrieving, practising and fixing. That's normal. That's what working looks like.

## When it's a huge book and you're short on time

Sometimes the honest answer is that you won't finish. If that's where you are, a few things help:

- Read the chapter summary and the headings first, then the questions at the end. Now you know what the chapter is trying to teach you before you start. (More on this in [how to get through a textbook chapter fast](/blog/how-to-read-a-textbook-fast/).)
- Learn the definitions, diagrams and formulas cold. They're quick to memorise and they appear in almost every paper.
- Use past questions as your reading list. If you can answer every question from the last five years, you're in better shape than most of the room.

## The short version

- Look at past papers before the textbook.
- Sort topics into must, should and nice to know. Start with "must".
- Read once, then test yourself. Rereading is not studying.
- Come back to every topic at least two or three times before the exam.
- If you can't finish, cover the high-mark topics and past questions first.

You don't need more hours. You need the hours you have pointed at the right things.
