People who seem to learn fast usually aren't reading faster than you. Most of them read at a perfectly normal speed. What they do differently is waste less of the time after reading, the bit where the material either sticks or quietly drains out of your head.

So if you want to learn faster, the question isn't "how do I get through pages quicker". It's "how do I make each hour count for more". Here's what works.

## Know what you're trying to learn before you start

This sounds obvious, but most of us open a chapter and just start at the top. Try this instead. Spend two minutes on:

- The headings and subheadings
- Any summary or "key points" box
- The questions at the end of the chapter

Now you have a map. Your brain knows where things go when you meet them, and you'll notice the important bits instead of giving every sentence the same weight. It's the difference between walking into a city with a map and walking in blindfolded.

## Learn in small pieces, then check

Long, unbroken reading sessions feel serious. They're also where attention goes to die. Somewhere around page twelve you realise you've read the same paragraph three times.

Break things into chunks you can finish in 15 to 25 minutes. At the end of each chunk, close the book and answer one question: *what did that section just say?* Say it out loud or scribble it down. If you can't, you didn't learn it yet, you just looked at it. Go back to the part that's fuzzy, not the whole thing.

This tiny habit is the single biggest speed-up most students can make. It's called [active recall](/blog/active-recall-study-method/) and it's backed by decades of research.

## Connect it to something you already know

New information sticks better when it's hooked onto old information. When you meet a new idea, ask yourself:

- What is this like? (An analogy, even a silly one, is fine.)
- Where have I seen this before?
- What would happen if this weren't true?

If you're learning how blood flows through the heart, picturing it as a two-storey house with one-way doors is not childish. It's how memory works. The weirder and more specific the picture, the better it tends to stick.

## Use examples, not just definitions

A definition tells you what something is. An example shows you what it looks like in the wild, and that's what you'll face in an exam or in real life.

For every concept, try to find or make up two examples. For maths and physics, that means working problems, not reading solved ones. For history or economics, it means a specific event or case. If you can explain a concept with an example you invented yourself, you understand it.

## Review sooner than you think you need to

Here's the frustrating part about memory: you forget most new material surprisingly quickly unless you revisit it. The famous "forgetting curve" shows a steep drop in the first day or two.

The good news is that each review flattens the curve. A five-minute review the next day saves you an hour of relearning later. Then a few days later. Then a week. If you want a ready-made schedule, we put one together in [spaced repetition for students](/blog/spaced-repetition-schedule/).

Learning fast, it turns out, is mostly about not having to learn the same thing twice.

## Sleep is part of studying

This one gets ignored, especially in exam season. But a lot of the work of locking memories in happens while you sleep. Pulling an all-nighter to learn more often means you remember less of what you learned. If you must study late, keep it to review, and still get a decent amount of sleep. There's a whole post on [studying the night before an exam](/blog/study-the-night-before-exam/) if that's where you are.

## Teach it to someone (or pretend to)

Explaining something forces you to find the holes in your understanding. When you get stuck mid-sentence, that's a gap. Fix it, then try again.

You don't need an actual audience. A friend works, a sibling works, your reflection works. Some people record a voice note explaining the topic and listen back. It's awkward the first time and genuinely useful every time.

## Quick recap

1. Skim the headings and questions first so you know what matters.
2. Work in 15 to 25 minute chunks and recall after each one.
3. Hook new ideas onto things you already know.
4. Use examples you make up yourself.
5. Review the next day, then a few days later, then a week later.
6. Sleep. Seriously.
7. Explain it out loud to find the gaps.

Do these consistently for a couple of weeks and you'll notice the difference. It won't feel faster at first. It'll just feel like you remember more, which is the same thing.
