You sit down to study. You open the book. Your phone buzzes. Twenty minutes later you've watched six videos, replied to three chats and somehow ended up reading about deep sea fish.

This isn't a willpower problem, or not only one. Phones and apps are built by very clever people whose job is to keep you looking. Fighting that with pure discipline is a bad trade. It's much easier to change the setup so focusing takes less effort.

## Put the phone in another room

Not face down. Not on silent next to you. Another room.

Studies on this are pretty striking: just having your phone visible on the desk can reduce how well you concentrate, even when it's switched off. Part of your brain keeps checking whether you should check it.

If you need it for an alarm or timer, use a cheap kitchen timer or your laptop instead. If you need it for an emergency, leave the ringer on in the other room. You'll hear it.

## Study in short, timed blocks

Long open-ended sessions ("I'll study all afternoon") are where focus falls apart. Short, defined blocks are much easier to stick to.

The most popular version is the Pomodoro technique:

1. Pick one task.
2. Set a timer for 25 minutes.
3. Work on only that task until it rings.
4. Take a 5 minute break.
5. After four rounds, take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes.

You don't have to use exactly 25 minutes. Some people prefer 40 or 50. The point is having a finish line close enough that you can stay focused until you reach it. "I just have to get to the timer" is much easier than "I have to study for five hours".

## Decide what you'll do before the timer starts

A lot of lost focus comes from not knowing what you're supposed to be doing. You open your notes, look around, feel overwhelmed, and reach for the phone.

Before each block, write down one specific goal. Not "biology" but "answer the 10 questions at the end of chapter 4". Not "revise maths" but "do problems 1 to 8 on integration". A clear goal gives your brain something to grab onto.

## Make breaks actual breaks

Scrolling isn't really a break. It's more input for an already tired brain, and it's very hard to stop after five minutes.

Better options:

- Stand up, stretch, walk around
- Get water or a snack
- Look out of a window
- Do nothing at all for a few minutes

Save your phone for the long break, and use a timer for that too.

## Write distractions down instead of acting on them

Halfway through a session you suddenly remember you need to reply to someone, or look something up, or buy a charger. Instead of doing it, write it on a scrap of paper next to you and keep going. Deal with the list in your break.

This works surprisingly well. Your brain relaxes once the thought is written down somewhere safe.

## Make the work active

It's hard to stay focused when you're passively reading. Your eyes move, your mind wanders. Active tasks keep you engaged: answering questions, solving problems, explaining things out loud, drawing diagrams from memory.

That's one more reason to swap rereading for [active recall](/blog/active-recall-study-method/). It's not just better for memory. It's also much harder to zone out while doing it.

## Look after the basics

Focus is easier when you've slept, eaten, and had some water. That's not exciting advice but it's true. If you're struggling to concentrate every day, it's worth looking at sleep first. Studying tired is like running with weights on.

## Use apps that block other apps

If you really can't leave your phone alone, use its own tools against it. Focus modes, screen time limits, and app blockers can lock distracting apps during study hours. On a laptop, browser extensions can block specific sites while you work.

## The short list

- Phone in another room.
- Short timed sessions with one clear goal each.
- Real breaks, not scrolling.
- Write distractions down.
- Do active work, not passive reading.
- Sleep.
