Textbooks are not written to be read like novels. They're dense, repetitive in places, and full of side boxes that may or may not matter. If you read every line from top to bottom at the same pace, a 30-page chapter can eat an entire evening and leave you with very little.

Here's a method that gets most students through a chapter in about an hour, with better understanding than a slow read. No speed reading tricks involved, just reading in a smarter order.

## Step 1: Look at the end first (5 minutes)

Flip to the end of the chapter. Read the summary, the key terms list, and the review questions.

This tells you what the author thinks matters. Those questions are basically the exam the chapter is preparing you for. Once you've seen them, you'll recognise the important parts as you hit them instead of guessing.

If your teacher gave you specific questions or a topic list, look at those too.

## Step 2: Skim the structure (5 minutes)

Now go back to the start and flip through without really reading. Look at:

- Headings and subheadings
- Bold and italic terms
- Diagrams, tables and their captions
- The first sentence of each section

You're building a mental table of contents. By the end, you should be able to say roughly what the chapter covers in a few sentences, even if you don't understand the details yet.

## Step 3: Read section by section, with a question in mind (30 minutes)

Turn each heading into a question. "The Causes of the French Revolution" becomes "What caused the French Revolution?" "Newton's Second Law" becomes "What does the second law say and how do I use it?"

Now read the section to answer that question. You'll find you can move quickly through background and examples you already get, and slow down on the parts that actually answer it.

Some practical tips while you read:

- **Don't highlight much.** If you must, highlight only the one sentence per paragraph that answers your question.
- **Study the diagrams.** In science subjects, the diagram often explains the idea better than the text around it.
- **Work the examples.** In maths, physics or chemistry, cover the worked solution and try the problem yourself first.
- **Skip the side stories on a first pass.** "Did you know?" boxes are interesting but rarely examined. Come back if you have time.

## Step 4: Close the book and recall (10 minutes)

This is the step that makes the whole thing stick. Close the book. On a blank page, answer the questions you made from the headings. Then try the review questions at the end of the chapter.

Check your answers. Anything you couldn't answer, go back to that section only, reread it, and try again.

This is [active recall](/blog/active-recall-study-method/), and it's what turns "I read the chapter" into "I know the chapter".

## Step 5: Make a tiny summary (5 minutes)

Write a short summary on one page or one index card: the main ideas, key terms, and any formula or diagram worth remembering. Keep it short. This is what you'll use for later reviews, not the whole chapter.

## Total: about an hour

That's 5 + 5 + 30 + 10 + 5. You can stretch the reading section for harder chapters, but the shape stays the same. And because you've already tested yourself, the next review (ideally the following day, see our [spaced repetition schedule](/blog/spaced-repetition-schedule/)) will go much faster.

## What if the whole book is due?

Sometimes it isn't one chapter. It's the entire textbook and the exam is close. In that case:

1. Use past papers and the syllabus to pick the chapters that matter most.
2. Do steps 1, 2 and 4 for every chapter, even if you skip step 3 on the less important ones.
3. Spend your deep reading time on foundational chapters, the ones later chapters build on.

This is how you get a working understanding of the whole book without reading every page. It's also, honestly, what most top students do.

## Quick version

1. Read the summary and end-of-chapter questions first.
2. Skim headings, bold terms and diagrams.
3. Turn headings into questions and read to answer them.
4. Close the book and answer the questions from memory.
5. Write a one-page summary for later review.

Try it on your next chapter and time yourself. Most people are surprised how much faster it goes.
