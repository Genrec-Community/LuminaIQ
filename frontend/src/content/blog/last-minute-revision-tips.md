Last minute revision has a bad name, but it's not the enemy. The final few hours before an exam are when facts are freshest, and a good last-minute session can lift your marks. The trick is using the time on the right things.

This guide is for the hours before the exam, or the morning of it. If you have a whole evening, read our guide on [studying the night before an exam](/blog/study-the-night-before-exam/). If you have two or three days, try the [rush exam plan](/blog/exam-in-2-days-rush-plan/).

## The rule for the last few hours: revise, don't learn

There's a difference between *revising* something you've seen before and *learning* something brand new. In the last few hours, revision wins every time.

New, complicated topics take time to understand and even longer to remember. If you try to learn one now, it will probably be gone by the time you turn over the paper, and you'll have lost the time you could have spent sharpening things you half-know.

So your job is simple: take what you already sort-of know and make it solid.

## Step 1: Make a one-page "last look" sheet (20 minutes)

Grab a single sheet of paper and fill it with the things most likely to earn quick marks:

- Definitions that are asked word for word.
- Formulas, with what each letter means.
- Key diagrams, roughly sketched and labelled.
- Lists: causes, effects, stages, advantages.
- Dates, names and numbers you keep mixing up.

Writing this sheet *is* revision. Deciding what goes on it forces you to think about what matters.

## Step 2: Cover and recall (30 to 45 minutes)

Now, cover the sheet and try to write it out from memory. Check. Fix what you missed. Do it again.

Two or three rounds of this beat an hour of reading, because you're practising the exact skill the exam needs: pulling information out of your head. That's [active recall](/blog/active-recall-study-method/) in its quickest form.

## Step 3: Rapid-fire questions (30 minutes)

Use past paper questions, end-of-chapter questions or flashcards. Don't write full answers. For each question, say out loud what you'd write: the key point, the term, the formula, the example.

If you can say it, move on. If you can't, put a mark next to it and look it up. Come back to marked questions at the end.

## Step 4: Rehearse one long answer (15 minutes)

If the exam has essays or long-answer questions, plan one in five minutes: headings, key points, examples. You don't need to write the whole thing. You're rehearsing the structure, which is where most marks in long answers come from.

## What to skip in last minute revision

- **Rereading whole chapters.** Too slow, too passive.
- **Rewriting neat notes.** Looks productive, isn't.
- **Brand new difficult topics.** Low return in the time you have.
- **Comparing with friends.** "Did you do chapter 7?" only spreads panic.
- **Hours of videos.** One short clip to clear a doubt is fine. Autoplay is not.

## The last 30 minutes before the exam

- Read your one-page sheet once, slowly.
- Eat something and drink water.
- Use the toilet.
- Pack your pens, calculator, ID and admit card the night before, and check again.
- Stop revising about 15 minutes before you go in. Breathe. Your brain needs a moment to settle.

## In the exam hall

Before you start writing, jot down the formulas and lists from your sheet in the margin or on rough paper (if allowed). Now they're out of your head and safe. Then read the whole paper, start with the questions you're most confident about, and keep an eye on the clock.

## Last minute revision checklist

1. Don't learn new hard topics. Revise what you half-know.
2. Make a one-page sheet of definitions, formulas, diagrams and lists.
3. Cover it and recall it, two or three times.
4. Answer questions out loud, rapid-fire.
5. Plan one long answer.
6. Stop 15 minutes before and breathe.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is last minute revision effective?

Yes, if it's revision and not new learning. Reviewing material you've already seen, and testing yourself on it, keeps it fresh for the exam. Trying to learn large new topics in the final hours is much less effective.

### How do I revise a whole subject at the last minute?

You don't cover all of it equally. Use past papers and the syllabus to pick the highest-mark topics, make a one-page summary, and test yourself on that. Accept that some low-priority topics will be skipped.

### Should I study right before an exam?

A light review of a one-page summary is helpful. Heavy studying in the last 15 minutes usually adds stress without adding marks. Give your brain a short break before you go in.

### What is the fastest way to revise?

Testing yourself. Covering your notes and recalling them, or answering practice questions, is faster and more effective than rereading or highlighting.
