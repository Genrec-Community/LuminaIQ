Studying fast isn't about reading faster. It's about cutting out the parts of studying that don't earn marks, so every hour counts.

Most students lose huge amounts of time to habits that feel like work but aren't moving them forward. Fix those and you'll cover more ground in less time, without the "I studied it but forgot it" problem. Here's how.

## Where study time usually goes to waste

Before speeding up, look at where the time goes:

- **Rereading** pages you've already read.
- **Making perfect notes** that copy the textbook.
- **Studying topics you already know** because it feels good.
- **Switching** between subjects, apps and tabs.
- **Phone breaks** that turn five minutes into thirty.

Cut these and you've often freed up half your study time.

## 1. Know what you're aiming for

Speed starts with direction. Look at the syllabus and a couple of past papers before you start. Focus on high-mark topics and the question types that actually appear. Our guide on [what to study for an exam](/blog/what-to-study-for-exam/) shows how to prioritise in 20 minutes.

## 2. Skim first, then read with a question

Spend two minutes skimming a section: headings, bold terms, diagrams, summary. Then read with a question in mind, like *"what are the three stages of this process?"* Reading to answer a question is much faster than reading to "take it all in".

## 3. Take short notes from memory

After each section, close the book and write the key points in a few lines. That's both your notes and your first round of recall. It's faster than copying, and you'll remember more.

## 4. Test yourself straight away

The fastest way to learn something is to try to recall it. Answer a few questions right after you study a section. You'll find out in two minutes whether you know it, instead of discovering it in the exam. This is [active recall](/blog/active-recall-study-method/).

## 5. Spend time on what you get wrong

When you test yourself, sort topics into "know it" and "not yet". Then spend your time only on the "not yet" pile. This one habit can halve the time you need, because you stop studying what's already solid.

## 6. Use timed blocks

Work in 25 to 45 minute blocks with a clear goal for each one: "finish section 3.2 and answer five questions". A deadline makes you work faster. A break after each block keeps you sharp. If focus is the problem, see [how to focus while studying](/blog/how-to-focus-while-studying/).

## 7. Don't multitask

Switching between tasks feels efficient but costs time every time you switch. One subject, one topic, one tab. Phone in another room.

## 8. Review quickly, but on schedule

Fast studying fails when you forget everything a week later. Avoid that with short, planned reviews: a couple of minutes on each topic after a day, a few days, and a couple of weeks. It's the cheapest way to make your fast studying stick. See our [spaced repetition guide](/blog/spaced-repetition-schedule/).

## Fast study plan for one chapter (about an hour)

| Minutes | What you do |
|---|---|
| 0 to 5 | Skim headings, diagrams, summary, end-of-chapter questions |
| 5 to 30 | Read section by section, a question in mind |
| 30 to 40 | Close the book, write key points from memory |
| 40 to 55 | Answer practice questions, mark the ones you miss |
| 55 to 60 | Fix the misses, make flashcards for them |

For more on getting through dense chapters, read [how to read a textbook fast](/blog/how-to-read-a-textbook-fast/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How can I study faster and remember more?

Skim before reading, take short notes from memory, test yourself straight away, and spend most of your time on what you get wrong. Short spaced reviews keep it in memory.

### How do I finish a big syllabus quickly?

Prioritise by marks using the syllabus and past papers, study in timed blocks with a clear goal, and skip rereading and copying notes. Accept that low-priority topics may get less time.

### Does speed reading help with studying?

Not much. Comprehension usually drops when you read too fast. Skimming for structure, then reading with a question in mind, is a more reliable way to save time.

### Is it possible to study one chapter in one hour?

For most chapters, yes, if you skim first, read with focus, recall from memory and practise with questions. Very dense or technical chapters may need two sessions.
