If you only change one thing about how you study, make it this one.

Active recall means pulling information out of your memory instead of putting it back in. Instead of reading your notes again, you close them and try to remember what they said. That's it. It sounds too simple to matter, and it's one of the best-supported ideas in the whole field of learning research.

## Why rereading tricks you

When you reread a page, it feels easy. The words are familiar, the ideas make sense, and you get a nice sense of "yeah, I know this".

The problem is that familiarity isn't the same as memory. You recognise the material because it's right in front of you. Tomorrow, in the exam, it won't be. You'll have a blank page and a question, and you'll need to *produce* the answer. That's a completely different skill, and rereading barely trains it.

Highlighting has the same issue. It feels active, but mostly you're deciding which sentences look important, not learning them.

## Why recall works

Every time you successfully drag a fact out of your memory, you make the path to it a little stronger. The next time, it's easier to find. Researchers call this the testing effect, and it's been shown again and again, across ages, subjects and types of exams.

Here's the part that surprises people: recall works even when you get it wrong. Trying to remember, failing, then checking the answer still helps more than just reading the answer. The struggle is part of what makes it stick.

That's also why it feels worse. Rereading feels smooth. Recall feels hard, slow and a bit embarrassing. That difficulty is a sign it's working, not a sign you're bad at it.

## Five ways to use active recall today

### 1. The blank page

After you read a section, close the book. Take a blank sheet and write down everything you can remember. Headings, ideas, examples, diagrams, anything. Then open the book and check. Mark what you missed in a different colour. Those gaps are your next study session.

### 2. Questions instead of notes

When you make notes, write them as questions. Instead of "Photosynthesis happens in the chloroplasts", write "Where does photosynthesis happen?" with the answer hidden or on the back. Your notes become a quiz you can take again and again.

### 3. Past papers, early

Don't save past papers for the last week. Use them from the start. Try a question before you feel ready, see what you can do, then study the gaps. It's uncomfortable, and it's very efficient.

### 4. Flashcards (done right)

Flashcards are active recall in its simplest form. Two rules make them work: say or write your answer *before* you flip the card, and be honest about whether you got it. If you just flip and think "yeah, I knew that", you're back to rereading.

Pair flashcards with [spaced repetition](/blog/spaced-repetition-schedule/) and they get much more powerful.

### 5. Explain it out loud

Pick a topic and explain it as if you're teaching it to a friend who missed class. No notes. When you hit a spot where you go "um... it's kind of like...", that's a gap. Go back, fix it, try again.

## How much recall versus reading?

A good rough rule: for every bit of time spent taking in new information, spend at least as much time recalling it. Many strong students lean even further, reading once and spending most of their time on questions.

If you're used to reading for two hours straight, try this instead: read for 20 minutes, recall for 10, and repeat. You'll cover slightly less ground per hour, and remember a lot more of it.

## Common mistakes

- **Peeking too early.** Give yourself a real go before checking. Thirty seconds of trying is worth it.
- **Only testing what you know.** It feels good to quiz yourself on easy stuff. The point is to find the hard stuff.
- **Not checking answers.** Recall without feedback can lock in mistakes. Always check.
- **Doing it once.** One round helps. Several rounds spread over days is where the big gains are.
