You open the textbook, look at the contents page, and freeze. Twelve chapters. Where do you even start?

Knowing *what* to study is half the battle. Two students can put in the same hours and get very different marks, simply because one spent their time on the topics that actually came up. Here's how to be that student.

## Start with what the exam rewards

Your goal isn't to know the textbook. It's to score marks in a specific exam. So the first question is: *what does this exam reward?*

You can find out from four places.

### 1. The syllabus or exam blueprint

Many boards and universities publish the syllabus with marks or weightage per unit. If a unit is worth 20 percent of the paper and another is worth 5 percent, you know where your time should go.

### 2. Past papers

Past papers are the best guide you have. Go through the last three to five years and tally which topics appear and how many marks they carry. You'll quickly see a pattern: a handful of topics show up almost every year.

### 3. Your teacher

Teachers usually give hints, even when they don't mean to. Topics they spent the most time on, came back to, or called "important" are good bets. Go back through your class notes for these.

### 4. Your own weak spots

A topic you already know well needs less time than one you've barely understood, even if both are worth the same marks.

## Build your priority list

Make a quick table with every topic and give each one two scores from 1 to 3:

- **Marks:** how much it's worth and how often it appears.
- **Gap:** how weak you are on it right now.

Multiply them. The highest scores are what you study first.

| Topic | Marks (1-3) | Gap (1-3) | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|
| Photosynthesis | 3 | 3 | 9 |
| Cell division | 3 | 1 | 3 |
| Plant hormones | 1 | 3 | 3 |
| Respiration | 2 | 2 | 4 |

This takes 20 minutes and can save you days.

## Study foundations before details

Within each topic, learn the basics before the hard parts. Key terms, the main idea and the simple version of a process come first. If you start with the complicated bits, nothing sticks because there's nowhere for it to stick to.

When subjects build on each other (maths, physics, chemistry, accounting), check that earlier topics are solid before later ones. A shaky foundation is why later chapters feel impossible.

## What to study in the last week vs the last day

- **Weeks before:** work through your priority list properly, with practice questions and [spaced reviews](/blog/spaced-repetition-schedule/).
- **The last week:** past papers, then fix the gaps they show. Light review of strong topics.
- **The last day:** definitions, formulas, diagrams and a one-page summary. No big new topics. See our [last minute revision](/blog/last-minute-revision-tips/) guide.

## Signs you're studying the wrong things

- You've spent hours on a topic that's worth a few marks.
- You keep rereading chapters you already know because it feels nice.
- You haven't looked at a single past paper yet.
- You're making notes on everything instead of testing yourself on the important things.

If any of these sound familiar, go back to your priority list.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I know which topics are important for an exam?

Check the syllabus weightage, tally topics across past papers, and note what your teacher emphasised. Topics that appear often and carry high marks are the most important.

### What should I study first for an exam?

Start with high-mark topics you're weak on. Within each topic, learn the foundations (key terms and core ideas) before the detailed parts.

### Should I study everything in the syllabus?

If you have time, cover it all, but not equally. Spend most of your time on high-weightage topics. When time is short, it's better to master the important topics than to skim everything.

### How do I use past papers to decide what to study?

List every question from the last few years by topic and marks. Patterns appear quickly. Then practise those question types under timed conditions.
