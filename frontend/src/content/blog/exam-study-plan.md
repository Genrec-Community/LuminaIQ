Most study plans fail on day two. They're too ambitious, too rigid, or so pretty that making them took longer than following them.

A good plan is simple, a bit boring, and built around the time you actually have rather than the time you wish you had. Here's how to make one in about half an hour.

## Step 1: Write down every exam and its date

Start with the facts. List each subject, the exam date, and roughly how much it's worth to you (some exams matter more than others; be honest).

Then count the days you have until each one. Take out days you know you can't study much, like family events, travel, or other deadlines.

## Step 2: Break each subject into topics

For each subject, list the topics or chapters from the syllabus. Next to each, mark:

- **How important it is** (based on past papers and how often it shows up)
- **How well you know it right now** (good, okay, or not at all)

The topics that are both important and weak are where most of your time should go. Important and strong topics need review. Unimportant and weak ones go to the bottom of the list.

If you're not sure what's important, our post on [how to study for exams when you have too much to cover](/blog/how-to-study-for-exams/) explains how to use past papers to figure it out.

## Step 3: Estimate time, then add a buffer

Guess how long each topic will take to learn properly. Then multiply by 1.5. Everyone underestimates. Everyone.

Add it all up and compare it to the days you have. If it doesn't fit (it usually doesn't), cut from the bottom of the priority list, not the top. It's better to know 80% of the syllabus well than 100% badly.

## Step 4: Plan backwards from the exam

Start with the exam date and work back:

- **The last 2 to 3 days:** past papers and review only. No new topics.
- **The week before that:** finish remaining "must know" topics and keep reviewing.
- **Everything earlier:** learn new topics, highest priority first, with short reviews built in.

Planning backwards makes sure you don't end up learning new material the night before.

## Step 5: Build in reviews from the start

Every topic you learn should come back a few times before the exam. The easy way: when you schedule a topic on a day, also pencil in a 10 minute review the next day, and another one a few days after that. This is [spaced repetition](/blog/spaced-repetition-schedule/), and it's the difference between learning something and still knowing it in three weeks.

## Step 6: Plan days in blocks, not hours

Instead of "study 9am to 9pm", plan in blocks:

- **Block 1:** new topic (the hardest thing, while you're fresh)
- **Block 2:** practice questions
- **Block 3:** reviews of earlier topics
- **Block 4:** lighter work, like making a summary sheet

Three or four focused blocks a day is plenty for most people, especially if you're also in school or college. A block can be 45 to 90 minutes. Mix subjects across the day if you have several exams; switching keeps things fresher than six hours of one thing.

## Step 7: Leave one empty slot a week

Something will go wrong. You'll get sick, or a topic will take twice as long, or you'll just have a bad day. Leave one block or half-day each week with nothing in it. That's where you catch up. Without it, one bad day knocks the whole plan over.

## If you're starting late

Say the exam is in a week and you're just starting. The same steps apply, just compressed:

1. Spend an hour on past papers and the syllabus to find the highest-mark topics.
2. Plan two or three topics per day, most important first.
3. Every evening, quickly review that day's topics. Every morning, review yesterday's.
4. Keep the last day for past papers and your summary sheets.
5. Sleep properly. Really.

If the exam is tomorrow, skip to our guide on [studying the night before an exam](/blog/study-the-night-before-exam/).

## The 30-second version

- List exams and dates.
- Rank topics by importance and how well you know them.
- Estimate time, add 50%, cut from the bottom.
- Plan backwards: last few days are review only.
- Schedule reviews for every topic.
- Plan in blocks and leave a free slot each week.

A plan doesn't need to be perfect. It just needs to tell you what to do when you sit down, so you spend your energy studying instead of deciding.
